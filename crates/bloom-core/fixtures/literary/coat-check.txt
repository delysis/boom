The museum had lost every coat except the one nobody wanted to claim.

Inez counted the empty hooks twice. It was important to be certain before calling Leo, who would make a distinction between a missing coat and an absent coat, and then ask whether the owner had been notified of the distinction. There were nineteen hooks, a bent umbrella stand, and a green wool coat with a lining the colour of mustard. She had put it on hook nineteen herself at eleven that morning. Its owner had given her a pound coin and said she would not need a ticket.

The visitors had left during the power cut. Inez remembered the short woman with a bicycle helmet, the man who kept asking whether the portrait room was upstairs, the schoolchildren pouring out through the fire exit. She had held open the door. Until the lights returned, coats had seemed less urgent than people.

At four, Leo arrived with two cups of coffee and a roll of raffle tickets.

“For next time,” he said.

“There won't be a next time.”

“That's what makes it a precaution.”

He set the coffee on the desk beside the only surviving claim ticket. It was a square of heavy cream card, stamped 19. Inez had found it under her chair. Someone had folded it once along the diagonal, then flattened it again. The crease ran through the numeral like a tiny road on a map.

She turned the ticket over. On the back, in blue ink, was an address she knew: their mother's old house, now divided into three expensive flats. Below it, someone had written the word Tuesday.

Leo stopped unwinding the raffle tickets.

“Did you write that?”

“Why would I?”

“You sometimes put things where you'll find them.”

“So does everybody. That's what putting things means.”

She meant to sound irritated. Instead she sounded like their mother explaining the kitchen to a visitor. Leo looked at the coat. Their mother had owned a green coat, but hers had been short, with horn buttons. This one came nearly to the floor and had a black ribbon sewn inside the collar. It was not the same coat. Inez was certain of that and annoyed to have become certain of it.

Footsteps crossed the gallery above them. Ms Voss, the museum director, appeared at the foot of the stairs carrying a bowl of imitation lemons. She put it down on the desk, where it displaced Leo's coffee.

“The shop needs its display back,” she said. “Have you found the missing items?”

“We have found the remaining item.”

Ms Voss touched the green sleeve with two fingers. “That belongs to the woman who came to see me. She left through my office.”

“And did she mention our mother's house?” Leo asked.

Inez had not meant to show Ms Voss the ticket. Leo had already picked it up. The director read the address, but her expression changed only when she turned the card back to the stamped number.

“That's not one of ours,” she said.

Leo looked at Inez. She held out her hand for the ticket.
