June came down to the pumping station carrying the map in a cake tin. Her sister Éva was already there, crouched beside the locked door, picking burrs from the hem of her trousers. Neither had brought the key. The station had closed before their mother died, and the locks had been changed afterwards, though the water still came up the channel every evening as if someone remained on duty.

“You said it would be open,” Éva said.

“I said we used to leave it open.”

Éva dropped the last burr into the grass. The grass was short and stiff here, bent inland by a wind that had now gone elsewhere. Beyond the wall, the reservoir held a dull strip of sky. June could hear water touching the lower steps. It was not yet high enough to reach the mark their mother had painted on the concrete, a blue line that had faded to the colour of a vein beneath skin.

She put the cake tin on the doorstep. Inside it the map had kept its smell of the kitchen cupboard: flour, old paper, the sharp ghost of a lemon. Their mother had drawn the shoreline in black ink and made corrections in red. Whenever the water took another piece of bank, she had scratched out the old edge. What remained was a coastline with a fringe of small wounds.

Éva lifted the map by its corners.

“She's put the apple tree on the wrong side.”

“She moved it.”

“You can't move an apple tree.”

“She moved where she drew it.”

There had been two apple trees. June did not say so. She could see both of them clearly, though she could no longer remember which one had borne fruit. Their mother standing between them with a washing basket, a towel hanging from her wrist; that was the picture she had kept. The trees themselves were only a way of holding it upright.

Éva smoothed the map across her knees. Where the paper had worn thin, daylight showed through the reservoir. June remembered tracing that same pale shape with her finger while her mother read figures over the telephone. She had been small enough to believe that making a place pale on paper could let light into the place itself.

“There,” Éva said.

In the margin, beside the station, was a red cross neither of them remembered. It was not a correction to the shoreline. Their mother had drawn a narrow path down from it, through the reed bed, to a square labelled shed. The shed had burned the summer June left for university. She remembered smoke over the reservoir and the scrape of grit inside her shoes. She had not gone back to look at what remained.

“That path is under water now.”

Éva held the map higher, putting the paper's reservoir against the real one. It was a childish gesture, and for a moment June felt the familiar tenderness of being allowed to see her sister do something without explaining it.

Behind the station, beyond the rushes, a metal surface caught the light. June had taken it for a loose piece of roof. Now she could see that it stood upright. The red path ran towards it.

She put the empty cake tin beneath her arm. Éva folded the map once, along the crease their mother had made.
